# Onion client authorization

Status: revision 4. Revision 3 shipped in Android 3.0.12; revision 4 adds
the partial-commit rules, the lost-key recovery and the `AUTH_READY`
generation rule of section 5, changes no record format and is not yet
released. iOS port pending (section 10). The control-port primitives
(`TorControl`, `TorOnionServiceControl`) and everything above them follow
this document.

## 1. Why

A contact address is a Tor v3 onion service. Without client authorization
Tor lets anyone who holds the address complete the introduction and open a
circuit; Zerion then refuses the connection within its first 16 bytes unless
the peer presents a recognition tag derived from the transport keys shared
with one paired contact. That gate is sound but sits above Tor: an address
holder can confirm the service is reachable and make it accept connections.

Tor v3 client authorization moves the first refusal into Tor: the service
descriptor is encrypted for a set of X25519 client public keys, and a client
without a matching private key cannot decrypt it, cannot learn the
introduction points and cannot open a circuit. Precisely: an unauthorized holder of the onion address cannot
decrypt the protected descriptor layer that carries usable
introduction-point information and cannot establish an authorized
connection to the service. No stronger claim (that the service's existence
is unobservable) is made or relied on.

## 2. Keys: directional, owned by the dialer

Each direction of a contact pair has its own client key pair. For the pair
(A, B) there are two: `k(A->B)`, generated by A, whose public half B
registers on B's authorized service and whose private half only A holds;
and `k(B->A)` the other way round.

- A generates `k(A->B)` at random when authorization is offered to B and
  stores the private half in its encrypted database with the contact
  record, like the contact's transport keys.
- The public half travels to B inside the authenticated, end-to-end
  encrypted per-contact transport-properties group, the same channel that
  carries the onion address itself. Nothing else can substitute it: a
  substituted key would have to be delivered through a channel that is
  already authenticated by the contact root key.
- No private key is ever shared or derived from shared material.

Why not one deterministic pair per contact derived from the root key:
that design was simpler (no message, nothing stored) but it puts the same
private key on both endpoints, so a compromise of either device reveals the
keys of both directions, no direction is isolated, and a stale backup
restores keys that were meant to be gone. Tor's model is that the client
owns the private key and the service holds only public keys; the
directional design follows it. Evaluation:

| Property | Shared deterministic | Directional, dialer-owned (chosen) |
|---|---|---|
| Compromise blast radius | both directions of every contact of the compromised device | only the compromised device's own dialing keys; peers' keys for dialing it stay private to the peers |
| Directional isolation | none | full |
| Revocation | republish service without the key | same, plus the revoked peer's private key is useless without its pairing |
| Rotation | change a label version | generate a new pair, deliver the public half, republish |
| Backup and restore | keys re-derivable from the root key at any age | keys restored with the encrypted database; a stale backup carries stale keys, handled in section 6 |
| Key substitution | not applicable | prevented by the authenticated channel |
| One contact compromised | reveals nothing about others (keys are per contact either way) | reveals nothing about others |
| Complexity | lowest | one message type, one table column |

## 3. Per-contact state machine

State is stored per contact in the database (a table keyed by contact id,
added by a schema migration; a missing row means `LEGACY`). Transitions are
monotonic except revocation.

    LEGACY            no authorized path with this contact
    AUTH_NEGOTIATING  both sides advertise support; keys exchanged, services published
    AUTH_CONFIRMED    this side proved the authorized path in at least one direction
    AUTH_REQUIRED     both sides committed; legacy is closed for this pair for good
    REVOKED           contact removed or blocked; terminal

Rules:

- `LEGACY -> AUTH_NEGOTIATING`: both sides advertise `onion3auth` and this
  side has sent `AUTH_OFFER` (section 5).
- `AUTH_NEGOTIATING -> AUTH_CONFIRMED`: `AUTH_READY` received and this
  side's probe succeeded (a dial that reached the peer's `onion3a`, or a
  recognised connection from the peer through our authorized service), or
  the peer's `AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS` was received.
- `AUTH_CONFIRMED -> AUTH_REQUIRED`: this side has sent `AUTH_COMMIT` and
  received the peer's `AUTH_COMMIT`.
- `AUTH_CONFIRMED` with `AUTH_COMMIT` sent (a partial commit): the peer may
  already have accepted that `AUTH_COMMIT` and be `AUTH_REQUIRED`, which
  it cannot leave. So this side dials `onion3a` only, keeps the peer's key
  on its authorized service and its own credential for the peer's
  `onion3a`, and is never cleared by the pre-commit cleanup (section 5).
  It still accepts the peer over either service, because it is not
  `AUTH_REQUIRED` itself. It converges by the repair of section 5; when
  it hears nothing over the authorized path it also gets the open repair
  dial and, if the peer lost its keys, a new round that keeps the
  committed one (section 5, Lost peer keys).
- `AUTH_REQUIRED`: dial `onion3a` only. A failed client-key registration is
  a hard failure (no dial). An unreachable `onion3a` is retried with backoff
  and never replaced by `onion3`. An inbound session from this contact that
  arrives over the open service is recognised by its tag and then closed:
  it is not a downgrade path. No message from the peer moves this state
  anywhere but `REVOKED` (a local action). The one bounded exception on the
  outbound side is the open repair dial of section 5 (Lost peer keys),
  which looks for a peer that lost its keys; it never changes the dial
  target, the state or the inbound rule.
- Re-keying: the peer sent a new key after this side committed (section 5,
  Lost peer keys). The state stays what it was, `AUTH_REQUIRED` or
  `AUTH_CONFIRMED` with `AUTH_COMMIT` sent, with its inbound rule, while
  a new round runs underneath it. The committed round stays usable until
  the new one has proven itself: the committed peer key stays on the
  authorized service and the committed address stays the dial target. The
  round ends at the next mutual `AUTH_COMMIT`, or by returning to the
  committed round when the peer shows that it still holds it.
- `* -> REVOKED`: contact removed or blocked, section 7. A contact re-added
  later is a new pairing with a new root key and is meant to start at
  `LEGACY`. Known limitation: the record is keyed by the internal contact id,
  which the database reuses when the most recently added contact is removed
  and another is added; the new contact then inherits the `REVOKED` record,
  inbound connections from it are refused, and its stream counters start
  from the removed contact's values.
- Before `AUTH_REQUIRED` the pair is at today's posture: open service gated
  by the recognition tag. Nothing is weaker than today at any point.

The state, both key pairs' relevant halves, the peer's `onion3a`, the peer's
public key and the negotiated version live in the encrypted database, so
they survive process restart, Tor restart (Tor is re-fed from the database,
section 8), database restart and upgrade (migration adds the table; absent
row equals `LEGACY`, which is exactly the pre-upgrade behaviour).

## 4. Two services during migration

A Tor service is either open or authorized for every client. Requiring
authorization on the existing address would cut off every contact whose app
lacks support, including iOS until its port ships. So a device publishes:

- `onion3`, open, for contacts not yet in `AUTH_REQUIRED`;
- `onion3a`, authorized, whose descriptor is encrypted for the public keys
  of every contact in `AUTH_NEGOTIATING` or later.

Support is advertised with the transport property `onion3auth=<version>`.
`onion3a` itself is not a broadcast property: it is sent per contact inside
`AUTH_OFFER`, so a contact that never negotiated never learns it (section
6, linkability).

The open service is retired for a pair at `AUTH_REQUIRED` (inbound from
that contact closed, outbound dialled only by the bounded open repair dial
of section 5). Retiring the open service for
the whole device is a deliberate later step, taken when every contact is
`AUTH_REQUIRED` or `REVOKED`; it is not automatic.

## 5. Activation protocol

Messages are records in the existing per-contact transport-properties
group: authenticated, end-to-end encrypted, ordered and de-duplicated by
the sync layer. Each carries `version` (protocol version, 1) and a
`keyVersion` (section 6).

    AUTH_OFFER          { version, keyVersion, my onion3a, my client public key for your service }
    AUTH_READY          { keyVersion }   "your key is on my onion3a; your onion3a and my credential are installed in my Tor"
    AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS  { keyVersion }   "an authenticated session over your onion3a succeeded"
    AUTH_COMMIT         { keyVersion }   "I have my probe success and yours; legacy closes for me now"
    AUTH_ROTATE         { keyVersion, new onion3a and/or new client public key }
    AUTH_ROTATE_ACK     { keyVersion }

In every record but `AUTH_ROTATE_ACK`, `keyVersion` is the sender's own
generation; `AUTH_ROTATE_ACK` carries the generation it acknowledges,
which is the receiver's. Versions up to 3.0.17 put the receiver's
generation in `AUTH_READY` and compared it with the sender's, so a pair
whose two generations differ (one side has rotated its authorized
address, after removing a contact, on request or after 30 days) never
accepted each other's `AUTH_READY`, never probed and never activated; it
went back to `LEGACY` at the seven-day cleanup and started again. A side
now sends its own generation in `AUTH_READY`, which those versions accept
whenever they know this side's current generation, and accepts an
`AUTH_READY` that carries either the sender's generation or its own.

Sequence for a pair:

1. Both advertise `onion3auth`. Each side generates its dialing key for the
   other, registers the other's public key as soon as it has it, and sends
   `AUTH_OFFER`. State `AUTH_NEGOTIATING`.
2. On receiving `AUTH_OFFER`: republish `onion3a` with the new public key
   in the set, install the peer's `onion3a` plus own private key in Tor
   (`ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_ADD`), send `AUTH_READY`.
3. On `AUTH_READY`: probe. Only one side of a pair dials (the designated
   dialer, fixed by the author identifiers; the only exceptions are the
   repair dials under Partial commit and Lost peer keys below), so the
   probe is directional. The designated dialer is asked to dial the peer at once,
   past any backoff, and the transport reports which address the dial
   reached; a dial that reached the peer's `onion3a` is that side's probe.
   The other side's probe is a recognised connection from the peer
   (transport tag recognised) that arrived through this device's
   authorized service, which proves the peer's credential and this
   device's service together. A connection that reached the open address,
   or that arrived through the open service, is never a probe. On its
   probe a side sends `AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS`. State `AUTH_CONFIRMED`.
   A probe still open after a Tor restart is dialed again after the
   re-feed.
4. A side that holds its own probe success and the peer's sends
   `AUTH_COMMIT`. On receiving the peer's `AUTH_COMMIT` while having sent
   its own: `AUTH_REQUIRED`. A peer sends `AUTH_COMMIT` only after its own
   probe, so an `AUTH_COMMIT` that passes every check below but arrives
   before the peer's `AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS` counts as that probe success.
   The sync layer does not keep the two records in order: received
   records are validated concurrently, and a record sent in a session
   that drops before it is acknowledged is sent again only after its
   retransmission delay (at least two minutes over Tor), so an
   `AUTH_COMMIT` created later goes out first in the next session. An
   `AUTH_COMMIT` that arrives before this side's own probe succeeded still
   changes nothing.

Invariants: legacy is never closed for a pair before both directions are
proven (`AUTH_COMMIT` requires both probe successes). Legacy is never
reopened after `AUTH_COMMIT`. Every message is bound to the contact by the
channel, so no third party and no downgrade message from the peer can move
the state backwards.

Asymmetry and recovery. A side in `AUTH_REQUIRED` that receives a state
message from a lower state re-sends `AUTH_COMMIT`, paced as described under
Partial commit below. A side in a lower state
that receives `AUTH_COMMIT` moves to `AUTH_REQUIRED` only when every one of
these holds; otherwise the message changes nothing (it has still been
delivered, so the sync layer does not send it again):

- the message version is supported;
- its `keyVersion` equals the current negotiation or rotation generation
  on this side, not older and not newer;
- the peer's `onion3a` persisted for that generation is the one the
  session used or the one announced for it;
- the fingerprint of the peer's client public key in the message matches
  the persisted one for that generation;
- this side's own dialing-key generation for the peer matches;
- the record arrived over the authenticated per-contact channel of that
  contact (the channel provides this; a record from any other group is not
  a state message);
- the contact is not `REVOKED`.

A stale `AUTH_COMMIT` (an older generation, including one replayed from a
restored backup) and a future one (a generation this side has not reached)
are ignored and noted in the state as a repair trigger. When the local
state cannot establish these invariants the side fails closed, attempts an
authenticated repair by re-sending its own current state message. A peer
that lost its key state entirely (a backup older than the negotiation, or
a partial commit cleared by an earlier version) cannot reach us over
`onion3a` and we refuse it over `onion3`; we do not reopen legacy. The
pair is recovered without re-pairing by a new round of keys over a session
that the contact's own keys authenticate (Lost peer keys below).

Partial commit. One side can be `AUTH_REQUIRED` while the other has sent
`AUTH_COMMIT` but rejected or not yet received the peer's (an
`AUTH_COMMIT` rejected for a generation or address that changed in
flight, or one that arrived early on a version that did not yet count it
as a probe success). The two sides then repair it with bounded records:

- The side with the partial commit sends `AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS` and
  `AUTH_COMMIT` again one hour after its commit, then after doubling
  delays capped at one day, at most ten times per generation. A rotation
  on this side restarts the count, since it makes the earlier records
  stale; a rotation announced by the peer only moves the next repair one
  hour later and adds none, so a peer cannot extend the repairs by
  rotating. It also re-sends `AUTH_COMMIT` whenever the database is opened.
  A locked-in peer answers the `AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS`; a peer with a
  partial commit of its own accepts the `AUTH_COMMIT`.
- A side that locks in re-sends its `AUTH_COMMIT` once, one hour later,
  for a peer that rejected the first copy. A pair locked in by an earlier
  version does this once at the first check after the update.
- Each of those repairs and that one re-send also asks the transport for
  one repair dial: a single dial, past any backoff, to the peer's
  `onion3a`, made even by the side that is not the designated dialer, so
  the records reach a peer that never dials this side. Apart from the
  repairs of a new round of keys (Lost peer keys below) and the silence
  repair (next item), nothing else asks for it (not a probe, not an
  answer), so a side makes at most one repair dial after locking in and at
  most ten per generation with a partial commit. The peer accepts the
  dial: a locked-in peer through its authorized service, a peer with a
  partial commit through either.
- Silence repair. A locked-in side with no contact over the authorized
  path in either direction for a day (neither its own dial reaching the
  peer's `onion3a` nor the peer arriving through this side's authorized
  service) asks for the same repair dial, then again after 2, 4, 8 hours
  and so on, at most once every seven days, on a schedule of its own that
  any contact over the authorized path resets. It reaches a peer that is
  the designated dialer but cannot reach this side, and delivers the
  queued records. Up to 3.0.17 that included a peer that dialled an
  address this side had retired after the peer missed its `AUTH_ROTATE`;
  from 3.0.18 that address stays published until the peer has confirmed
  the new one (section 6). A healthy pair makes none; a peer silent for 30 days gets
  about ten.
- A locked-in side answers `AUTH_OFFER`, `AUTH_READY` and
  `AUTH_PROBE_SUCCESS` with its `AUTH_COMMIT` at most once an hour, and
  answers an `AUTH_COMMIT` only while it has not re-sent its own since
  locking in. An `AUTH_COMMIT` can be another locked-in side's answer, so
  answering every one would make two locked-in sides send `AUTH_COMMIT`
  back and forth for as long as both are online. Versions up to 3.0.17
  answer every one: after a restart repairs a partial commit between two
  of them, the surplus answers go back and forth without end. Against
  such a peer this side is the one that stops.

Versions up to 3.0.17 cleared a partial commit at the seven-day cleanup
below. Their peer had locked in and refuses the open service, so the pair
lost each other: at once when the side with the partial commit was the
designated dialer (it went on dialing the open address), seven days after
the negotiation started when it was not (the dialer reached it through
its `onion3a` until the cleanup removed that). Updating either side
repairs a partial commit that has not been cleared yet. When the peer on
such a version is the designated dialer and holds the partial commit, it
dials only the open address, which the locked-in side refuses; the
updated locked-in side then reaches it with the repair dial that comes
with its one re-send, through the peer's `onion3a`, which still
authorizes it. A pair whose partial commit was already cleared is a
lost-key case: it recovers once the side that stayed locked in runs this
version (Lost peer keys below), and not before.

Lost peer keys. A peer can lose the keys of a committed round: a version
up to 3.0.17 cleared its partial commit at the seven-day cleanup, or the
peer restored a backup older than the negotiation. Its side of the pair is
then `LEGACY`, or negotiating again with a new dialing key, while this side
is `AUTH_REQUIRED` or holds a partial commit (it sent `AUTH_COMMIT`, which
the peer never answered). This side's dials to the peer's `onion3a` fail (the
peer no longer authorizes its key) and the peer's dials to this side's
open service are refused. The contact itself is intact: both sides still
hold its root keys, and every session between them is authenticated by
those keys in the ZWF handshake, whichever onion service carries it. The
BLE mesh carries only chat and group records, not these records, so it is
no channel for this. Recovery runs over a Tor session instead:

- Open repair dial. A locked-in side, or a side with a partial commit, with
  no contact over the authorized path for a day and an hour (one hour
  after the first silence repair of a locked-in side, so the two dials
  never coincide) asks the transport for one dial to the
  peer's open address, made from either side of the pair; then again after
  2, 4, 8 hours and so on, at most once every seven days, until the
  authorized path works again. With the first one of
  a silent period it also sends its current `AUTH_OFFER` once. Dialing
  out to the open address is safe. The address comes from the peer's
  authenticated transport properties, only the holder of that onion key
  can answer, and the ZWF handshake then authenticates the peer's root
  key; client authorization protects the service side (who can reach and
  confirm a service), not the dialing side, and the peer publishes its
  open service anyway. A session forms only if the peer accepts this side
  on its open service, which a peer that is still `AUTH_REQUIRED` for the
  pair refuses, so a network that blocks `onion3a` gains nothing and an
  intact pair cannot be downgraded through it. This side's inbound rule
  does not change at any point. Because that refusal is expected, an open
  repair dial that closes before a frame authenticates is not counted
  towards marking the contact's keys out of sync (ZWF-ROOT-EVOLUTION.md);
  one that authenticates clears the mark as any dial does.
- Detection. An `AUTH_OFFER` whose client public key differs from the key
  this side committed with, arriving over the authenticated per-contact
  channel, means the peer may have discarded the committed round: a
  locked-in peer never sends `AUTH_OFFER`, and a negotiating peer repeats
  its key. No one but the contact can produce it, and no trust is placed
  in a key that did not arrive that way. It is not proof, though: the sync
  layer does not keep records in order, so an `AUTH_OFFER` from an
  abandoned earlier negotiation can arrive after the pair locked in. The
  new round is therefore built so that it can never lose a pair that
  still works.
- Re-keying. On that signal this side keeps its state (`AUTH_REQUIRED`, or
  `AUTH_CONFIRMED` with its partial commit) and its own dialing key, and
  starts a new round: the peer's new key goes on this side's authorized
  service next to the committed key, the credential is installed for the
  peer's `onion3a`, then `AUTH_OFFER` and `AUTH_READY`, the probes and
  `AUTH_COMMIT` as in steps 1 to 4. The committed peer key stays
  authorized and the committed address stays the dial target until the
  round's probe succeeds; only the round's mutual `AUTH_COMMIT` removes the
  committed key. The peer runs an ordinary negotiation and needs no change,
  so a peer on 3.0.15 to 3.0.17 recovers as well. The round is never
  cleared by the seven-day cleanup.
- Back to the committed round. An `AUTH_COMMIT` that matches the committed
  round (the committed peer key's fingerprint, this side's address and
  generations), or an `AUTH_OFFER` that carries the committed peer key,
  shows that the peer still holds that round. This side then drops the new
  round and its key and returns to the committed state; a partial commit
  answered that way locks in. A healthy pair that received a late
  `AUTH_OFFER` thus answers the round's `AUTH_OFFER` and `AUTH_READY` with
  its `AUTH_COMMIT` (every version does) and ends up where it was, over
  the path it never stopped using. An earlier key is not ignored outright:
  a peer that restored a backup can hold one again, and acting on it is
  now harmless and limited to once a day.
- Round repairs. Until the authorized path has worked since the round
  started, the open repair dial continues on its schedule; the round's
  `AUTH_OFFER` and `AUTH_READY` are repeated on the bounded repair
  schedule above, with one repair dial each, and a partial commit inside
  the round is repaired as above. A new round starts at most once a day
  per contact, so a peer
  cannot make this side republish its service or dial without limit. For the same reason a peer's client key, once this side
  holds one, is replaced at most once a day, inside a round and in an
  ordinary negotiation alike: an `AUTH_OFFER` with yet another key
  within the day changes nothing, and the peer repeats its key with its
  next offer. The authorized service is shared by every contact and a
  new key changes its authorized set, so without this limit one contact
  could take the service down for all of them with every record.
- What still needs an update. The side that stayed locked in must run this
  version. A locked-in side up to 3.0.17 never dials the open address and
  answers a new `AUTH_OFFER` with its old `AUTH_COMMIT`, and the cleared
  side holds no key for its `onion3a`, so nothing reaches it. Once it is
  updated, the pair recovers in about a day, without re-pairing. Re-adding
  an existing contact would not help on its own: it keeps the client
  authorization state of both sides as it is. Removing the contact on
  both sides and adding it again does reset that state, but it is not
  needed for this.

Pre-commit cleanup. `AUTH_REQUIRED` is irreversible except by local
revocation. `AUTH_NEGOTIATING` and `AUTH_CONFIRMED` are not: a negotiation
in which this side has not sent `AUTH_COMMIT` within seven days, or that
the user resets, is aborted locally (a side that has sent `AUTH_COMMIT`
holds a partial commit and is kept, and so is a new round that keeps a
committed one): the temporary dialing key pair for
the peer is deleted, the peer's public key is removed from the authorized
set and the service republished, the credential for the peer's `onion3a`
is removed from Tor, the persisted negotiation row is cleared, and the
pair is back at `LEGACY`, which is exactly today's posture. Abandoned
negotiations therefore never leave keys or authorized-client entries
behind.

Unanswered offers. An `AUTH_OFFER` that has not been answered by the time
the database is opened again (no key from the peer has been recorded) is
sent once more with the same dialing public key and generation. Receiving a
duplicate offer changes nothing on the peer, so the resend is idempotent;
it covers an offer the peer could not process at the time, without waiting
for the seven-day abort.

## 6. Rotation of the authorized address

Client authorization does not remove linkability: a current contact knows
the stable `onion3a`; a removed contact remembers it; a compromised contact
can share it; a restored backup may reintroduce a retired one. So `onion3a`
is versioned and rotated.

- `keyVersion` is a per-device counter over (`onion3a`, service key). A
  rotation publishes a new service with the current authorized set
  (excluding revoked contacts), keeps the old one published, and sends
  `AUTH_ROTATE` to every contact at `AUTH_NEGOTIATING` or later.
- A contact installs the new address and credential, probes it, and
  replies `AUTH_ROTATE_ACK`.
- Evidence: a contact has confirmed the new address when its
  `AUTH_ROTATE_ACK` for the current generation arrives, or when its
  `AUTH_COMMIT` for the current generation names the current address (a
  contact that sets up client authorization after the rotation never
  learns the old address; one that locked in after the rotation under an
  earlier version, which did not record that, counts as well). Only that
  evidence, the contact's removal or the rule of 90 days of silence below
  lets the old address go; there is no other deadline (from 3.0.18).
- Ninety days of silence (from 3.0.18). On the phone that is not the
  designated dialer for a contact (the smaller author id dials), the old
  address stops waiting for that contact once nothing has been heard from
  it for 90 days: no authenticated session and no received message on any
  transport. The time is the later of the last authenticated session and
  `lastContactMs`, which a connection opened or a message received for
  that contact updates (at most once an hour). A contact with no recorded
  time is not treated as silent, and any contact within the 90 days
  restarts the clock. The designated dialer never stops waiting, so the
  other phone can always reach it at an address it kept and the pair
  cannot split; a removed contact is unaffected. The check runs at every
  15 minute check, and the connection details and the list in the network
  settings then show only the contacts still holding the address back.
  The silence is measured on the device clock, so a clock set 90 or more
  days ahead retires the old address on that phone at the next check;
  this cannot split the pair, since the designated dialer never stops
  waiting. A clock set back writes nothing: a last contact time lower
  than the one already stored is ignored, so the silence can only grow
  with the clock, never shrink below what was seen.
- NORMAL ROTATION (scheduled or manual): the old service stays published
  with the current authorized set and is deleted (`DEL_ONION`) once every
  contact has confirmed the new address. Up to 3.0.17 it was also deleted
  on a retirement deadline of 30 days, whether or not every contact had
  acknowledged. A rotation asked for while an old address is still
  published changes nothing, so there is never more than one old address.
- REVOCATION ROTATION: the revoked contact's key leaves every published
  service at once. The current service is published again under its own
  key with the remaining contacts' keys only and becomes the old address,
  and a new address is published for the same set and announced with
  `AUTH_ROTATE`; the old address is then deleted as in a normal rotation.
  When Tor refuses a step (a failed `DEL_ONION` makes the following
  `ADD_ONION` collide), the revocation stays owed and the check every 15
  minutes runs it again, so the key is gone at the latest at the next
  check while Tor answers. A revocation while an older address is still
  published publishes both addresses again without the revoked key and
  stays owed; the new address follows as soon as the older one is
  retired. Until then the remaining contacts are not moved to a fresh
  address at all, however many contacts are removed meanwhile, and a
  removed contact that knew the current address can still see that a
  descriptor is published for it (it cannot authenticate to any of these
  services, and its tag is no longer recognised). Up to 3.0.17 the old
  service was deleted at once, before the new address had reached anyone:
  two locked-in contacts that both removed someone while apart each held
  only the other's deleted address, refused the other's open repair dial,
  and lost each other for good.
- Versions: keeping the old address is done by the side that rotates or
  removes, so it protects a pair only when that side runs 3.0.18 or later.
  A side on 3.0.17 or earlier still deletes its old address after a
  removal at once and after any other rotation within 30 days; its peer,
  updated or not, then holds only a deleted address and, being locked in,
  is refused on the open address, so the pair has no session until the
  side that rotated or removed updates. Once it does, its own repair
  dials reach the peer, deliver the rotation and the pair recovers
  without pairing again (`OnionClientAuthPairTest`).
- A contact that missed the announcement still finds the old address and
  learns the new one over that session. A contact that has been away the
  whole time keeps the old address published and holds back the next
  rotation for as long as it stays a contact. When this side cannot be
  reached at all, the silence repair of section 5 dials the contact's own
  address after a day without contact over the authorized path, from the
  side that does not normally dial.
- A client public key in `AUTH_ROTATE` is ignored, and the rest of the
  record is processed: no version sends one, and honouring it would let a
  peer make this side take down and republish its authorized service with
  every record. A new client key travels only in a new round of keys
  (section 5, Lost peer keys), which starts at most once a day.
- Triggers: every contact revocation (mandatory), that is removing a
  contact this device authorizes; on a schedule, a random 28 to 35 days
  after the last rotation (drawn at each rotation and stored with the
  service; a fixed 30 days up to 3.0.17), only once the address before it
  is retired and, from 3.0.18, only once every contact holding a commit
  has been in contact since the previous rotation, on any transport (a
  session that authenticated or a message received from it; the rotation
  record travels the same way). A rotation on request
  exists in the manager (`rotateAuthorizedService`); release builds offer
  no screen for it: the "Rotate onion now" button in the network settings
  rotates the open address (`B4OnionRotation`), not this one. Development
  builds offer it as "Renew authorized address" among their lifecycle
  test steps.
- Crash recovery: the service record (current address and key, old
  address and key, generation, whether a revocation is owed) and each
  contact's acknowledgement flag are persisted, and every step is
  idempotent. A restart publishes the current and the old address again,
  runs an owed revocation first, and keeps the rotation records already
  queued for contacts that have not acknowledged; a publish that fails is
  tried again at the next check every 15 minutes.
- A restored stale backup carries an old `keyVersion`; peers at a higher
  version answer with their current `AUTH_ROTATE`, and a peer that had
  revoked us has no key for us at any version (section 7).

## 7. Revocation

On contact removal or block, in this order, each step persisted as done so
a crash resumes at the next one:

1. Atomically mark the contact `REVOKED` locally and deny it application
   traffic from that moment (its recognition tags are refused).
2. Close every live session of that contact (the connection registry knows
   them). Tor's control specification states that removing a service does
   not close existing connections; Zerion closes them itself.
3. Remove the credential for the revoked contact's `onion3a`
   (`ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_REMOVE`) and delete the private dialing key.
4. Publish the current authorized service again under its own key with an
   authorized set that excludes the revoked contact; it becomes the old
   address, kept for the remaining contacts (section 6).
5. Publish a new `onion3a` with a fresh service key and the same set (a
   revocation rotation, section 6). While an older address is still
   published, both are published again without the revoked key instead,
   and the new address follows once the older one is retired.
6. Distribute the new address to the remaining contacts with `AUTH_ROTATE`;
   the old address is deleted once each of them has confirmed it.
   Contacts that cannot be reached find the old address, or are dialled
   over their own authorized service and updated there.

The contact's transport keys are deleted with the contact as today.

The open address (`onion3`) is revoked as well, by the address rotation
(`B4OnionRotation`, from 3.0.15): the rotation is recorded as owed in the
transaction that removes the contact (`contactRemoved`), so a process killed
right after the removal still owes it, and it stays owed until it has run; a
rotation already announcing is abandoned and its next onion unpublished, and
the address the removed contact knew is retired once every remaining contact
has moved, checked at every hourly round and every session, and at once when
no contact is left. Up to 3.0.17 it was retired after 48 hours at most whether
or not every contact had moved, so two contacts that both removed someone
while apart could lose each other; from 3.0.18 there is no time limit
except that the phone that is not the designated dialer for a contact stops
waiting for it after 90 days without any authenticated session or received
message on any transport (the designated dialer never does), and
until every remaining contact has moved the removed contact can still see
that the old open address is published and can still connect to it, where
the handshake refuses it because its tag is no longer recognised. Each hourly
check runs whatever happened to the ones before it.

The rotation is recorded as pending in the service record before it is
attempted and cleared only once the new service is published and announced
to the remaining contacts. A revocation while Tor is down, or a rotation
whose publish fails, is therefore completed at the next check every 15
minutes or at the next Tor start, before the service is re-fed; the revoked
key is never published again, and the old address is republished only with
the remaining contacts' keys.

A revoked client that still holds a decrypted descriptor can reach the old
introduction points until the descriptor expires; those lead to a service
being deleted and, at the app layer, to a tag that is no longer recognised.
Regression tests cover both the closed sessions and the refused tag.

## 8. Tor semantics, confirmed on the shipped binary (0.4.9.12, re-confirmed on 0.4.9.13 and 0.4.9.14)

Observed on 2026-09-24 against the Tor 0.4.9.12 that 3.0.12 ships and on
2026-09-25 against the Tor 0.4.9.13 built in tree for the next release, both
on the Moto, over the app's own authenticated control port (replies observed by
the device check, not logged by the app). On 2026-10-19 the same behaviour was
re-confirmed on the Tor 0.4.9.14 built in tree: forced address renewal in both
directions between two phones, aged lifecycle timers, 27 minutes of deep sleep,
a force stop and a Tor restart, all without re-pairing. These observations used a loopback
target; since 3.0.15 the target is a Unix socket in the
app's private directory (`Port=80,unix:<files>/zo/<name>`), which Tor's
ADD_ONION parses like a HiddenServicePort target. That form is not yet
confirmed on a device:

- `ADD_ONION NEW:ED25519-V3 Flags=Detach,V3Auth ClientAuthV3=<key>
  Port=80,127.0.0.1:<port>` returns `250-ServiceID`, `250-PrivateKey`, one
  `250-ClientAuthV3` line per key and `250 OK`, and the service appears
  under `GETINFO onions/detached`. Without the `V3Auth` flag Tor answers
  `512 No auth type specified` and publishes nothing; the flag is
  mandatory. Several `ClientAuthV3` entries per service are accepted.
  Re-adding a service whose key is still published answers `550 Onion
  address collision`, so a key-set change is `DEL_ONION` first, then
  `ADD_ONION` with the same key; the address is unchanged. `DEL_ONION` of
  an unknown service answers `552 Unknown Onion Service id`. Between
  those two commands the service is unpublished for every contact, so the
  manager republishes only when the authorized set or the address differs
  from what it last published successfully to the running Tor; every
  attach to a started Tor republishes. A record from a peer (an
  `AUTH_ROTATE`, a repeated `AUTH_OFFER`) therefore no longer takes the
  service down unless it changes the set, and a peer changes the set with
  a new key at most once a day (section 5, Lost peer keys).
- Capacity is bounded by the descriptor size limit (50 000 bytes); each
  authorized client adds one padded `auth-client` line to the
  superencrypted layer, which gives room for several hundred clients, far
  above any contact list here. The implementation caps the set and refuses
  beyond the cap rather than let Tor fail the upload.
- `DEL_ONION` removes the service from this Tor instance and does not close
  existing connections (control specification); section 7 closes them.
- `ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_ADD <56-char address> x25519:<base64 private key>`
  (bare address, no `.onion` suffix; with the suffix Tor answers `512
  Invalid v3 address`) observed: `250 OK` added; `251 Client for onion
  existed and replaced` when a credential for that address already exists,
  whichever key; `512 Failed to decode x25519 private key` for a malformed
  or short key; `552 Unrecognized key type` for a key type other than
  x25519. The classification is exhaustive: 250, 251, 252 (cached
  descriptor decrypted) succeed; 451 is the authorized-client capacity;
  512, 551 (client-name conflict, not reachable without `ClientName`), 552
  and any other reply, positive or negative, fail. Nothing unknown is
  treated as success. Credentials stay non-permanent (memory only);
  nothing is written to Tor's state directory. `ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_VIEW`
  lists the credential as `250-CLIENT <address> x25519:<key>`.
- `ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_REMOVE <address>` observed: `250 OK`; `251 No
  credentials for "<address>"` when nothing was registered, which the
  implementation treats as already removed; `512 Invalid v3 address` for a
  malformed address.
- A detached service and non-permanent credentials do not survive a Tor
  restart; after every Tor start the app re-adds both from the database
  before any dial, and a dial to `onion3a` before that step is refused.
- Non-permanent credentials also do not survive a configuration change
  of the running process: every `SETCONF`, whatever the option and even
  when the value does not change, makes Tor rebuild its client
  authorization map from `ClientOnionAuthDir` and discard every credential
  added over the control port (`hs_config_client_auth_all` in
  `options_act`; observed on the Moto: a credential listed by
  `ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_VIEW` is gone right after `SETCONF
  ConnectionPadding=1`, while a `GETCONF` leaves it and the detached service
  stays published). The app changes the configuration on every
  connectivity change (`DisableNetwork`), on a network restart and when
  bridges are re-applied, so after each such change the transport tells the
  manager to install every credential again from the database
  (`refeedCredentials`). The service and the negotiation
  state are not touched by that step; a pair at `AUTH_REQUIRED` is never
  dialed at the open address while the credential is missing, so the gap
  between the change and the re-installation is a failed dial, not a
  fallback.
- Cached descriptors are valid for at most their lifetime; rotation on
  revocation limits the window.

## 10. iOS port

Implemented (2026-09-24, iOS branch `feature/zwf-wire-port`). The protocol
lives in `Packages/ZerionProtocol/Sources/ZerionProtocol/OnionAuth/`:
`OnionAuthRecords` (the same six records, version 1, byte for byte, pinned
against Android by shared wire vectors), `OnionAuthRecord` and
`OnionAuthStore` (per-contact state and the device's service record in the
encrypted preferences, under the same key names Android uses),
`OnionAuthCommitCheck` (the same nine verdicts) and
`OnionClientAuthManager` (the state machine, the authorized service, its
rotation, revocation and the re-feed after every Tor start). The app side is
`TorOnionServiceControl` (the control commands, including the mandatory
`Flags=Detach,V3Auth` and the exhaustive reply classification) and
`OnionAuthCoordinator` (the encrypted store, the record sender over the
ordinary outgoing path so a record survives an offline contact, and the
hooks the dial path and the listeners call). The records travel in the
per-contact group of the `org.zerionproject.core.onionauth` client, computed
exactly as Android computes it. The partial-commit rules of section 5 (no
cleanup after `AUTH_COMMIT`, the bounded repair, paced answers, an early
`AUTH_COMMIT` counted as a probe success), its lost-key recovery (the
open repair dial and the new round of keys) and its `AUTH_READY`
generation rule change no record format and have to be followed by the
iOS manager as well.

Platform differences that do not change the protocol:

- The authorized service forwards to a second local listener
  (`PermanentListener.authorized`), so an inbound connection is tagged by
  the service it arrived through, as the Android transport does with its
  second accept loop.
- The probe is directional in the same way: the designated dialer's probe is
  a dial that reached the peer's `onion3a`, the other side's probe is a
  recognised connection through its own authorized listener. Which side
  dials is still decided from the addresses both sides publish openly, so
  substituting the authorized address as the dial target cannot flip that
  decision.
- iOS has no database transaction around the state changes; the manager
  applies them under one lock and serialises every Tor-mutating sequence on
  a gate, which gives the ordering the Android transaction gives.

Encodings: `ClientAuthV3` takes the 32-byte public key in base32 without
padding (52 characters, upper case); `ONION_CLIENT_AUTH_ADD` takes the
32-byte private key in base64 and the bare 56-character address.
